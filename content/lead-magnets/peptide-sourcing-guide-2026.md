# 2. What compounded peptides are (and aren't)
<!-- ✍️ DRAFT — fact-check, tighten my voice -->

A **peptide** is a short chain of amino acids — anywhere from 2 to about 50 of them, joined by peptide bonds. Anything longer is, by convention, called a protein. Insulin (51 amino acids) sits right at the boundary. BPC-157 has 15. GHK-Cu has 3.

That's the chemistry. What matters for sourcing is the *regulatory category* the peptide ends up in once a manufacturer makes it, and there are three categories you should be able to distinguish at a glance.

## The three grades

**Pharmaceutical grade** — manufactured under Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP), at facilities licensed by the FDA, EMA, MHRA, or equivalent. Purity is typically certified at ≥99% by HPLC, with full impurity profiling, sterility testing on every injectable batch, and a Certificate of Analysis (CoA) tied to a specific lot number. This is what your insulin is. It is what semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy) is when prescribed through a licensed pharmacy. Cost is high. Risk is low.

**Compounded (503A in the US; the analogous regulated pharmacy preparation in the UAE)** — prepared by a licensed compounding pharmacy from pharmaceutical-grade active pharmaceutical ingredients (API), against a specific patient's prescription, by a licensed pharmacist. The pharmacy is regulated, the source API is pharmaceutical-grade, and the preparation is sterile-tested. The compound itself is not an FDA-approved product, but it exists inside the formal medical system. This is the category Kamura focuses on for peptide therapy in the UAE.

**Research grade ("for research purposes only")** — sold through online vendors, typically with no enforced sterility standards, no chain of custody, and no clinical use intended. Purity claims are self-declared. The vials may be real peptide. They may also be the wrong peptide, the right peptide at the wrong dose, or contaminated with bacterial endotoxin from a non-sterile facility. The label *says* it isn't for human use. Most of it gets injected anyway.

The single most important sourcing decision a UAE patient makes is **which of these three categories your provider is actually drawing from**, regardless of what the marketing says.

## Why "compounded" exists as a category

The pharmaceutical industry only makes drugs at scale when there is a market large enough to justify the cost of full clinical trials and FDA approval. That leaves a long tail of compounds — older molecules, off-patent variants, small-population indications, and peptides that fall between research and approved drug — that have real clinical utility but no commercial sponsor.

Compounding pharmacies exist to fill that gap. A licensed compounding pharmacy can take pharmaceutical-grade raw materials and prepare them into a finished, sterile, traceable medication for an individual patient against a physician's prescription. Done properly, this is real medicine, made under real oversight, by real licensed pharmacists.

Done improperly — or by an unlicensed actor calling themselves a "compounding service" — it is none of those things.

This guide is about telling the difference.

## Common misconceptions worth clearing up

- **"Compounded means counterfeit."** No. Counterfeit means deceptive labeling and unknown contents. Compounded means licensed preparation from regulated ingredients. The two are opposites.
- **"If it's not FDA-approved, it's illegal in the UAE."** Also no. UAE peptide access is governed by MOHAP, DHA, and DOH-Abu Dhabi, not the FDA. A peptide can be unavailable as an FDA-approved drug and still be legally prescribed in the UAE through a licensed compounding pharmacy.
- **"Research grade is fine if it's the same molecule."** This conflates *identity* with *quality*. Two vials can contain the same peptide but differ wildly on purity, sterility, endotoxin load, and dosing accuracy. The molecule is not the product. The whole system is the product.

# 4. The 2026 reset — what the FDA changed and why it matters here
<!-- ✍️ DRAFT — sourced from your April 19 blog post. Fact-check and tighten. -->

If you have spent the last three years watching the peptide world get pushed underground, **April 15, 2026** was the day that started to reverse. On that date, the US FDA quietly published a notice removing twelve peptides from its Category 2 list — the "significant safety concern" bucket that had effectively blocked licensed US compounding pharmacies from preparing them since 2023.

This is a US action. The UAE is governed by MOHAP, DHA, and DOH, not the FDA. But the consequences land here quickly, and any sourcing decision you make in 2026 should account for what's happening on the regulatory side.

## The 12 peptides removed from Category 2

The substances officially removed are:

- BPC-157
- TB-500
- Cathelicidin LL-37
- Epitalon
- GHK-Cu (injectable)
- MOTS-c
- DSIP
- Dihexa
- PEG-MGF
- Melanotan II
- KPV
- Semax

The removal becomes effective seven days after publication. As of late April 2026, these twelve are no longer flagged by the FDA as posing a significant safety concern under the 503A compounding framework.

## What this *doesn't* mean

It does **not** mean these peptides are FDA-approved drugs. None of them are.

It does **not** mean US compounding pharmacies can immediately prepare them at scale. They cannot, yet.

It does **not** mean every clinic with a wellness Instagram account can offer a BPC-157 protocol on Monday morning.

What it means is that the door, slammed shut in 2023, is open again. The substances are now in regulatory limbo, awaiting formal review. The agency has not blessed them — it has stopped blocking them while it takes a closer look.

## Why the reversal happened

The FDA did not reach this conclusion on its own. The original nominators of these substances voluntarily withdrew their Category 2 nominations, which triggered the removal. This is a procedural reset, not new science changing the agency's mind. The actual scientific decision comes next.

## July 2026: the month that matters

The **Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee (PCAC)** is the body that will now formally review these compounds and recommend whether they belong on the 503A bulk drug substances list — the official menu of substances US compounding pharmacies can prepare.

The first PCAC meetings are scheduled for **July 2026** and will cover roughly half of the twelve peptides, including the two with the largest patient bases: **BPC-157 and TB-500**. The remainder are scheduled through early 2027.

If PCAC recommends a peptide for the bulks list and the FDA accepts, a US physician can write a prescription, a licensed compounding pharmacy can fill it, and the patient receives a product made under USP-grade conditions in a regulated facility. That is the difference between real prescription medication and a research chemical of unknown provenance.

If PCAC recommends against, the substance returns to a restricted state. The grey market keeps winning.

## What this means for UAE peptide access

UAE peptide access is governed by MOHAP, DHA, and DOH, not the FDA. Licensed UAE clinics have been prescribing peptides like BPC-157, CJC-1295/Ipamorelin, and Semaglutide through regulated compounding pharmacies throughout the US restriction window.

What changes for the UAE is **sourcing**. A more permissive US and European supply environment means four things in the next 12–18 months:

- **Lower active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) prices** for UAE compounding pharmacies, which should eventually flow to patient pricing
- **Broader Certificate of Analysis (CoA) availability**, making it easier for local clinics to verify batch quality
- **More published clinical data** as US research loosens up, improving evidence-based protocol design
- **Better practitioner training pipelines**, particularly for BPC-157 and TB-500, which have been under-taught for three years

If you are a UAE patient, the practical takeaway is simple: access was already legal here. What is arriving now is better-sourced product and deeper clinical familiarity.

## The honest disclaimer

The April 15 notice does not make research-grade peptides bought online safe. It does not change the fact that vials labeled "for research use only" are not manufactured under sterile conditions, not tested to pharmaceutical purity standards, and not traceable to a regulated batch.

Until a peptide lands on the 503A bulks list and your prescribing clinician can route it through a licensed compounding pharmacy, the safest path is the one the regulatory framework already supports: work with a qualified physician, demand a Certificate of Analysis, and source through a pharmacy that operates under MOHAP or DHA oversight.

The biggest risk in peptide therapy has never been the peptide itself. It has been the supply chain.

# 5. How a compounded peptide is actually made
<!-- ✍️ DRAFT — fact-check, tighten -->

When you receive a vial of compounded peptide in the UAE, it has passed through four hands before it reached yours. Understanding what should have happened at each step is how you tell a real supply chain from a fake one.

## Step 1 — Active Pharmaceutical Ingredient (API) manufacture

The starting material is the peptide itself, synthesised at a manufacturer's plant. For pharmaceutical-grade work, this means a facility certified to Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards — typically located in the US, Europe, China, or India, with documentation that withstands regulator inspection.

What you should be able to verify (your clinic should be able to tell you):
- The manufacturer's name and country
- GMP certification status
- Whether the manufacturer holds a Drug Master File (DMF) with the FDA or equivalent
- Whether the specific batch has a Certificate of Analysis showing purity, identity, and impurity profile

## Step 2 — Compounding pharmacy preparation

The API is shipped to a licensed compounding pharmacy. In the UAE, this is a pharmacy regulated by MOHAP, DHA, or DOH-Abu Dhabi, holding a compounding licence. Compounding is *not* simply repackaging — it is the preparation of a sterile, dosed, ready-to-administer medication, typically by lyophilization (freeze-drying) and reconstitution.

What should happen:
- The pharmacist works from a specific physician's prescription tied to a named patient (this is the regulatory definition of compounding)
- The peptide is dissolved in sterile bacteriostatic water at a known concentration
- Sterility testing is performed on each batch
- Endotoxin testing is performed (because peptides synthesised improperly can carry bacterial endotoxin even when the molecule itself is pure)
- A Certificate of Analysis specific to this preparation is generated, including: batch number, peptide identity confirmation, concentration, fill volume, sterility result, endotoxin result, and expiry date

What shouldn't happen but does:
- Bulk vials prepared in advance without a patient prescription on file
- "Research grade" API used because it was cheaper to source
- Sterility testing skipped or relied on the API supplier's documentation
- No batch-specific CoA provided to the clinician or patient

## Step 3 — Physician prescription

In the UAE, peptides can only be legally prescribed by a physician licensed by the relevant emirate authority (DHA in Dubai, DOH in Abu Dhabi, MOHAP elsewhere). The prescription should specify the peptide, dose, route of administration, frequency, duration, and the source pharmacy.

If the "clinician" prescribing your peptide is not a physician — or is a physician without a UAE licence — what you are receiving is not a prescription. It is an unregulated transaction wrapped in clinical language.

## Step 4 — Patient handoff and storage

The compounded peptide should be delivered with:
- The Certificate of Analysis for the specific batch
- Storage instructions (usually 2-8°C refrigerated, sometimes frozen)
- Reconstitution instructions (if delivered as lyophilised powder)
- Injection guidance specific to the molecule and dose
- An expiry date you can verify

If you receive a vial with no documentation, no batch number visible on the label, and no CoA — pause. Ask. If the clinic cannot produce these documents within a reasonable timeframe, you should not inject the contents.

## What "GMP-certified" actually means

GMP — Good Manufacturing Practice — is a set of internationally recognised standards for how pharmaceutical products are manufactured. Certification is granted by national regulators (FDA, EMA, MHRA, China NMPA, India CDSCO) after on-site inspection. A GMP-certified facility:

- Documents every step of the production process
- Maintains traceability from raw material to finished product
- Operates within validated environmental controls (air quality, temperature, particulate counts)
- Performs in-process and finished-product quality control testing
- Is subject to unannounced regulator inspections

"GMP-certified" is a verifiable claim. A clinic that uses the phrase should be able to tell you which authority issued the certification and when. A clinic that uses it as a marketing word, without being able to back it up, is telling you something.

# 6. The 8 questions every UAE clinic should answer
<!-- ✍️ DRAFT — designed to be torn out and brought to the appointment -->

Print this page. Bring it to the appointment. A clinic that hesitates on any of these questions is telling you to look elsewhere.

## 1. Where is the peptide sourced from? Name the manufacturer and the country.

**Acceptable answer:** A specific manufacturer name (e.g. "X Pharmaceuticals, Switzerland" or "Y Compounding Lab, Germany"), with documentation available.

**Unacceptable:** "We have multiple suppliers," "Our pharmacy partner handles that," vague references to "European-sourced" without naming names.

## 2. Which compounding pharmacy prepares it, and under what licence?

**Acceptable:** A named compounding pharmacy with a verifiable MOHAP, DHA, or DOH compounding licence (you can ask for the licence number).

**Unacceptable:** Generic answers about "our partner pharmacy" with no name, or pharmacies operating without specific compounding authorisation.

## 3. Can I see the Certificate of Analysis for the specific batch I'm receiving?

**Acceptable:** A CoA produced for *your* batch, with a batch number that matches your vial, dated within a reasonable window, including: peptide identity by mass spectrometry, purity by HPLC (≥99% for pharmaceutical grade), sterility testing result, and endotoxin testing result.

**Unacceptable:** A generic "specification sheet," a CoA from the API manufacturer without one from the compounding pharmacy, refusal to provide a CoA, or a CoA dated more than a year before your purchase.

## 4. Who is the prescribing physician, and are they licensed by DHA / DOH / MOHAP?

**Acceptable:** A named physician with a verifiable UAE medical licence number.

**Unacceptable:** "Our medical team prescribes," "We work with offshore physicians," any answer that doesn't terminate in a specific name you can verify on a public registry.

## 5. What's the protocol — dose, frequency, duration — and what's the evidence base?

**Acceptable:** A specific dose tied to your bodyweight or condition, a defined cycle length, an explicit reference to clinical trials or peer-reviewed literature supporting the protocol.

**Unacceptable:** "Most people take X" without context, dosing that varies wildly from published protocols without explanation, no awareness of what the underlying evidence is.

## 6. What are the known side effects and contraindications for *me* specifically?

**Acceptable:** A serious conversation about your medical history, current medications, and the specific risks of this peptide for someone in your situation. The clinician should be able to name 3-5 contraindications without checking.

**Unacceptable:** "Peptides are very safe," dismissal of risk, no review of your medications for interactions.

## 7. What happens if I have a reaction or want to stop?

**Acceptable:** A clear escalation path (their number, an after-hours protocol, what to do at the ER), no penalty or financial pressure for stopping mid-cycle.

**Unacceptable:** Vague reassurance, refunds tied to completing a course, no mention of how to reach the clinician outside the clinic.

## 8. What does this cost — all in — and what am I paying for?

**Acceptable:** A clear breakdown: consultation, peptide, compounding fee, administration if applicable, follow-up. Total should be within the typical UAE range for the molecule (see Part 8).

**Unacceptable:** Bundled "wellness packages" that obscure the per-vial cost, prices substantially below the market range (often a sign of research-grade product being passed as compounded), pressure tactics around package sizes.

# 9. The Kamura standard
<!-- ✍️ DRAFT — soft brand close. Tighten my voice. -->

Kamura was built because the gap between what's possible and what's safely accessible in UAE peptide therapy was too wide. The market is rich, the regulations are workable, and the patient demand is real. What was missing was a layer of curation that did the verification work most patients can't do for themselves.

The Kamura standard is the framework we apply to every clinic, pharmacy, and practitioner we list. It is what you receive when you go through us instead of going alone.

## What we verify before listing

**Licensing.** Every physician we work with holds a verifiable DHA, DOH-Abu Dhabi, or MOHAP licence. Every pharmacy holds a current compounding licence under the appropriate emirate authority.

**Supply chain.** We require named manufacturers, country of origin, and current Certificates of Analysis for every peptide on the formulary. No "European-sourced," no "our pharmacy partner handles that."

**Sterility and quality control.** Sterility testing and endotoxin testing on every injectable batch. Batch-specific documentation provided to each patient.

**Clinical literacy.** Prescribing physicians demonstrate fluency in the underlying literature for the peptides they prescribe. We don't list practitioners who learned about peptides last month.

**Pricing transparency.** Per-vial pricing must be disclosed alongside any bundled offering. Patients must be able to compare like-for-like across providers in our network.

## What we don't do

We don't list providers who refuse to disclose their sourcing.

We don't list "wellness clinics" without proper licensing, regardless of how good their Instagram looks.

We don't recommend research-grade peptides for human use under any circumstances.

We don't take fees from listed providers in exchange for higher ranking. Kamura Scores are independent of commercial relationships.

A vetted directory of UAE practitioners and pharmacies you can trust. A Kamura Score for every peptide based on evidence, safety, and value — independent of any commercial relationship. A personal dashboard that tracks your protocol, dosing, sessions, and outcomes over time. And, when our compounded peptide service launches in Q3 2026, a fully integrated path from physician consultation to delivered vial under one roof.
